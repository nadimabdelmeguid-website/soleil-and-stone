Trade shows are useful because they compress an industry into a physical space.

Within a few hours, you can walk past dozens or hundreds of companies, compare products that would normally be scattered across websites and sales decks, talk directly with the people behind them, and begin to understand what an industry is paying attention to.

But the most useful part of a trade show is not always the product being displayed.

It is the context around it.

Which booths are busy? Which technologies keep appearing? What are companies trying to differentiate on? What questions are people asking? And perhaps most importantly, is the market actually asking for what companies are building?

Those signals can sometimes tell you more than the announcements themselves.

## The Energy of the Floor Matters

One of the first things I notice when I arrive at a trade show is simply how busy it is.

That may sound superficial, but the number of people walking the floor can completely change the experience.

When a show is busy, exhibitors tend to have more energy. Their booths are active, conversations are happening continuously, and there is often a stronger sense that something meaningful is taking place.

That energy also changes the conversations you can have.

An exhibitor who has been speaking with customers, engineers, distributors, and potential partners throughout the day is often already thinking about the questions people are asking and the reactions they are getting.

When traffic is very low, the dynamic can feel different. Exhibitors may have more time to talk, but the overall energy of the floor can drop.

The atmosphere itself becomes a signal.

It does not tell you whether a technology will succeed, but it gives you an immediate sense of how much attention an event, market, or category is attracting.

## Repetition Shows Where Attention Is Going

At shows such as InfoComm or ISC West, many exhibitors already have established products on the market.

That creates an interesting environment because you are not only looking at early-stage concepts. You are seeing how existing companies are trying to improve products, reduce costs, increase productivity, or create a better experience for their customers.

One thing that becomes obvious very quickly is repetition.

You start seeing similar product categories across different booths. Similar features begin appearing in products from different companies. Similar language starts showing up in marketing materials.

That repetition is useful.

If dozens of companies begin incorporating the same technology, it tells you that the industry is paying attention to it.

Right now, one of the clearest examples is AI.

More and more hardware products are being presented as AI-enabled. That in itself is interesting because it shows where companies believe the market is moving.

But the presence of a technology across the show floor does not automatically mean the market is ready for every application of it.

That distinction matters.

## Technology Momentum Is Not the Same as Market Demand

When I look at an AI-enabled hardware product, the question that interests me is not simply whether the technology works.

I want to understand what it improves.

Does it make the product easier to use? Does it reduce cost? Improve productivity? Automate something customers are already struggling with? Create a noticeably better experience?

And, just as importantly:

**Is there a customer who is ready to pay for that improvement today?**

That is sometimes harder to answer.

At trade shows, you can see technologies moving through an industry before the commercial use case is completely mature. Companies are experimenting. They are exploring what new capabilities make possible. Some of those experiments will eventually become important products.

Others may not.

This is one of the most useful things a trade show can reveal: the difference between **technology momentum** and **market pull**.

A technology can be popular among exhibitors without yet solving an urgent problem for customers.

That does not necessarily make the technology bad. It may simply mean the market is earlier than the product.

## I Look for Differentiation

Because many companies at a trade show may be selling products in similar categories, I am usually less interested in seeing another version of something I already understand.

What interests me is how a company differentiates itself.

What are they doing differently from the ten booths around them?

Sometimes the answer is technical: better performance, lower power consumption, a different architecture, a new manufacturing approach, or a better integration of multiple technologies.

Sometimes it is commercial: a lower cost structure, easier deployment, a different business model, or a clearer focus on a specific customer.

And sometimes there is very little differentiation at all.

Walking the floor makes that much easier to see.

On a website, every company controls the environment around its product. The messaging is carefully written, the images are selected, and the product exists mostly on its own.

At a trade show, competitors are often standing a few meters apart.

The comparison becomes immediate.

## CES Feels Different

CES is a little different from many of the more specialized industry shows I attend.

The scale is enormous, and the biggest companies often have equally enormous booths. Those spaces can be impressive, but I do not always find them the most interesting.

I tend to be drawn toward smaller companies, niche products, and startup areas.

The reason is not simply that the products are newer.

It is because the conversation is different.

At a smaller booth, you may be speaking directly with the founder, CEO, CTO, or someone who has been deeply involved in creating the product. They can usually explain why it exists, what problem originally motivated it, what changed during development, and what they are still trying to figure out.

There is a story behind the product.

That personal connection makes it easier to understand the reasoning that led to what is sitting on the table in front of you.

A product from a large company might be the fiftieth product in a broad portfolio. For a startup, the product may represent years of work and much of the company's future.

Those conversations often reveal far more than the specifications.

## A Trade Show Is a Form of Field Research

I increasingly think of trade shows less as exhibitions and more as field research.

The goal is not just to see what exists.

It is to understand what people are trying to build, what companies believe customers will want next, and where different parts of an industry are converging.

You can observe which technologies are becoming common. You can see which features companies believe will differentiate them. You can talk to people who are actually building and selling the products. You can compare established companies with smaller challengers and see how each group approaches innovation differently.

And sometimes you can identify a gap between what the industry is excited about and what customers may actually be ready to adopt.

That gap is important.

Because when many companies begin moving in the same direction, it becomes easy to assume that following the trend is the safe decision.

It is not always.

## Building for a Need, Not a Trend

For founders and product teams, trade shows can create both inspiration and pressure.

You walk through hundreds of booths and see what everyone else is building. Certain technologies seem to be everywhere. Certain features suddenly feel unavoidable.

The natural reaction can be:

**We need that too.**

Sometimes that is the right conclusion.

But sometimes the more important question is:

**Why?**

What customer problem does it solve?

How urgent is that problem?

Does the technology materially improve the product?

Will customers understand the value?

And is the market mature enough to pay for it?

A trend can tell you where attention is moving. It cannot tell you whether your customer needs you to follow it.

That distinction becomes especially important in hardware, where product decisions can require significant engineering time, tooling, inventory, certification, supply-chain commitments, and capital.

Following the wrong trend is much more expensive when you have to manufacture it.

## Being First Is Not Always the Advantage

There is a strong tendency in technology to celebrate being first.

First to introduce a category.

First to integrate a new technology.

First to enter a market.

But being first also means taking on more uncertainty.

You may need to educate the customer. The infrastructure around the product may not exist yet. The use case may still be developing. Buyers may understand that the technology is interesting without yet seeing a reason to purchase it.

Sometimes the companies that arrive later benefit from all of that early experimentation.

They enter when customers better understand the problem, when the technology is more mature, and when the market has had time to decide which use cases actually matter.

Being first can be a significant advantage.

But only if the market is ready to follow.

## Final Thought

Trade shows are fascinating because they give you a glimpse of what industries are trying to become.

You see established products, experimental ideas, new technologies, competing approaches, and companies trying to anticipate where customers will go next.

That makes the show floor an excellent place to look for signals.

But signals should not be confused with answers.

Seeing the same trend across dozens of booths can tell you that companies are paying attention to it. It does not prove that customers are ready to buy it.

For anyone building a product, the lesson I take from that is simple:

> **Do not confuse industry momentum with customer demand.**

Pay attention to the trends. Understand the technology. Learn from what other companies are building.

But start with the market need.

Because the best product is not necessarily the one that reaches a new technology first.

It may be the one that reaches the customer **when the customer is ready for it**.
